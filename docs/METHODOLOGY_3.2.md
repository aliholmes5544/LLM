## 3.2.1 Sources and Rationale

The study evaluates detectors on published research data rather than on messages generated for the purpose, for four reasons. First, using the dataset of the most closely related study (Section 2.8) places the results on the same test material. Second, it separates the generating system from the detecting system: the spear-phishing emails were produced by a proprietary pipeline that is not among the open models evaluated here, so no detector is asked to recognize its own output. Third, it avoids producing new malicious content, a consideration developed in Section 3.9. Fourth, the contribution of this study lies in the evaluation, not in a new corpus; the benchmark it assembles adds value through controlled composition and derived variants rather than through generation. Both source repositories are pinned to a recorded commit hash, and every count reported in this section is produced by an audit script released with the code, so that the figures can be regenerated from the sources.

The core data come from the repository released with Nahmias et al. (2024), licensed under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. It contains four collections. The first is the set of LLM-generated spear-phishing emails produced by the authors' automated system; the paper reports 333 messages, and the pinned repository contains 334 records, all distinct, which are used in full. These emails are genuinely targeted: each addresses a named recipient in a defined role, refers to specific projects, events, or colleagues, and requests a concrete action. They are also homogeneous: all are set in a single fictional organization with a recurring cast of personas and a narrow thematic range centered on cybersecurity work and conferences. This homogeneity is recorded as a limitation and has two design consequences: no prompt, example, or threshold is developed on this collection, to avoid teaching a model its personas, and a second collection is needed to test whether findings extend beyond it. The other three collections supply the remaining classes: legitimate email from the Enron corpus (Klimt & Yang, 2004), legitimate but spam-like email from the SpamAssassin "hard ham" set, and a collection of traditional, human-written phishing distributed with the repository. These are the sources Nahmias et al. used. Because this study applies its own preprocessing, described below, and retrains the baselines itself, comparisons between detectors within this study are made under identical treatment of the data; comparison with the figures published by Nahmias et al. is indicative only, since their preprocessing and partitioning are not fully reproducible from the published description.

The second collection is E-PhishLLM (Pajola et al., 2025), released with the authors' ACM AISec paper and used here in its English portion: 11,502 emails, of which 5,996 are labeled phishing and 5,506 legitimate. It differs from the core data in ways that make it informative as a second test rather than a copy of the first. It was produced by a different generation pipeline, conditioned on synthetic user profiles rather than on a single organization; it spans many organizations, sectors, and writing styles; and it includes synthetic legitimate email of the same recency and register as its phishing, so that a detector cannot separate the classes by era or style alone. It also differs in a way that prevents merging: its phishing is not uniformly targeted. A coarse analysis carried out for this study, using the salutation as a proxy, finds that about 61 percent of the English phishing emails address a named recipient, about 32 percent use a generic salutation such as "Dear User," and about 7 percent have none. E-PhishLLM is therefore treated as a collection of LLM-generated phishing of mixed specificity, evaluated and reported separately from the spear-phishing core, with the salutation-based flag retained so that results can be broken down by it. What this second test can show is whether findings on the core set extend to a differently constructed collection of LLM-generated email; it is not evidence about real attack traffic, and no claim of that kind is made from it. The repository carries no explicit license; the data are therefore obtained by a download script and not redistributed with the study's code.

## 3.2.2 Preprocessing

All records are reduced to a common representation consisting of an identifier, the source collection, a binary class label, a finer-grained subclass label, a subject line, and a body in plain text. Only the subject and body are presented to detectors, in keeping with the scope of Section 1.9 and the threat model of Section 1.3; sender names and any other metadata present in the sources are discarded.

Inspection of the released files motivates several explicit rules. In the Nahmias et al. (2024) repository, every spear-phishing body is stored as HTML, whereas the legitimate and traditional collections are mostly plain text. HTML is therefore stripped from all collections with the same procedure: tags are removed, block-level elements and line breaks are converted to newlines, character entities are decoded, hyperlink targets are dropped while anchor text is kept, and whitespace is collapsed. This removes a formatting difference that would otherwise distinguish the positive class from the negative class on surface grounds alone. Field names are harmonized where the sources are inconsistent, records that cannot be parsed are excluded and logged, and a missing subject is represented as an empty string rather than dropped. Bodies longer than 600 words are truncated at the last sentence boundary before the limit and the number of truncated records is reported; the spear-phishing emails, whose bodies range from 59 to 324 words, are unaffected.

Duplication is handled across all collections jointly rather than within each. Exact duplicate bodies account for 1,225 of the 3,000 Enron records and 116 of the 490 hard-ham records, with smaller numbers in the traditional phishing collection and none among the spear-phishing emails. After normalization, exact duplicates are removed first; near-duplicates, defined as pairs whose sets of word trigrams have a Jaccard similarity of at least 0.80, are then removed, keeping one member of each cluster. The same test is applied after partitioning: no item in the test set, the controls, or the generalization set may have a near-duplicate in the baseline training set, the development set, or the pool from which few-shot examples are drawn, and the number of items removed at this stage is reported. Counts before and after each step are produced by the audit script.

E-PhishLLM requires one rule of particular importance. In its released form, hyperlinks and similar elements are replaced by placeholder tokens of the form `<<link>>`, and these tokens appear in about 60 percent of the English phishing emails and in none of the legitimate ones. Some variants are explicit, such as `<<link to fake login page>>`. Left in place, the placeholder would function as a label: a detector could identify most phishing in this collection by the presence of the token alone, and a language model would be told, in the text, that a link is fake. All placeholders are therefore replaced by neutral surface phrases that preserve grammaticality but carry no class information, for example "the link" for link placeholders and "the attachment" for attachment placeholders, and the replacement rule is released with the code. This treatment is consistent with the text-only scope of the study, under which URL content is not available to any detector in any collection.

## 3.2.3 Derivation of Paraphrased and Obfuscated Variants

Variants are derived from the 334 spear-phishing originals only. They model the adversary of Section 1.3, who refines a message to evade detection; legitimate senders do not. Two families of transformation are applied, each at two intensities, giving four conditions. Both are applied before any detector is run, with fixed random seeds, and both protect a fixed set of spans that must survive unchanged: personal and organizational names, dates, times, amounts, and any placeholder phrase. Protected spans are masked before transformation and restored afterward. Because every variant descends from one original, the variants are not independent observations; the original email is the unit of analysis throughout, each variant is paired with its original, and the statistical treatment in Section 3.7 is built on that pairing.

Paraphrasing rewrites sentences while preserving propositional content and the requested action. It is performed by an instruction-tuned open model drawn from a model family that is excluded from the detector set, so that no detector is evaluated on text produced by a close relative; the model and its decoding settings are fixed in Section 3.3. The model is instructed to rewrite a sentence in different words, to keep its meaning, tone, and level of formality, and to add no information. Intensity is defined by the proportion of sentences rewritten, selected with a seeded random draw: in the low condition, half of the sentences in each email are rewritten; in the high condition, all are. This operationalizes the adversarial refinement that Chen et al. (2026) describe.

Obfuscation alters the surface form of words while leaving the text readable to a person. It is performed by a deterministic script. Eligible tokens are alphabetic words of at least four characters that are not protected. Intensity is defined as the proportion of eligible tokens perturbed, 10 percent in the low condition and 30 percent in the high condition, each selected token receiving exactly one operation drawn uniformly, with a seed, from a fixed menu of four: substitution of one letter by a visually confusable character from a fixed homoglyph table; substitution of one letter by a conventional digit lookalike from a fixed map; insertion of an invisible zero-width space inside the word; and insertion of a visible separator inside the word. The script records every edit, with its position, original character, and replacement, in a log released alongside the variants. Obfuscation is a design decision of this study, as Section 1.3 notes, and these definitions and logs are what make it reproducible.

Because the fourth research question is answerable only if a variant still carries the original message, every variant is checked before use, and the residual risk that a check misses a changed message is estimated rather than assumed away. For paraphrases, three automatic tests apply to each email: the sentence-embedding cosine similarity between original and variant (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019) must be at least 0.80; the ratio of variant to original length must lie between 0.70 and 1.40; and every protected span must be present. In addition, each rewritten sentence is tested against its source with a natural language inference model, and the pair must be judged mutually entailing with probability at least 0.70 in both directions, which targets changes of intent that similarity alone can miss. These values are provisional: they are calibrated on the development set described in Section 3.2.4 so that at least 95 percent of paraphrases judged acceptable by the annotators pass, and the values finally used are stated with the code. A variant that fails is regenerated once with a new seed and excluded if it fails again. For obfuscated variants, readability is secured by construction, since each perturbed word receives a single edit from a menu of human-readable operations and no more than 30 percent of eligible words are touched; the automatic test is one of integrity, in which the edit log is applied in reverse and must reproduce the original exactly, confirming that nothing outside the logged edits was altered.

Manual review completes both checks. A stratified random sample of 10 percent of the variants in each condition is rated independently by two annotators on a three-point scale for whether the message's intent and requested action are preserved and, for obfuscated variants, whether the text remains readable; agreement is reported as Cohen's kappa (Cohen, 1960), disagreements are adjudicated, and any variant judged not preserved by both annotators is excluded. The failure rate observed in the sample is reported with a 95 percent confidence interval as the estimate of the residual rate in the unreviewed variants; if the sample failure rate in any condition exceeds 5 percent, every variant in that condition is reviewed. All exclusions are counted and reported.

Two control conditions accompany the variants. A paraphrased legitimate control applies the high-intensity paraphrase to a seeded sample of 334 legitimate emails from the test set, to establish whether paraphrasing as such raises the false positive rate. A modern legitimate control consists of 334 English legitimate emails from E-PhishLLM, to establish whether false positives on the core set depend on the age and register of the Enron and SpamAssassin material. Obfuscation is not applied to legitimate email, since the threat model gives legitimate senders no reason to obfuscate.

## 3.2.4 Composition and Partitioning

Table 3.2 summarizes the resulting data. The prompt-based detectors are not trained, so the whole core set is evaluation data for them. The core test set pairs the 334 spear-phishing originals with 1,002 legitimate emails, a ratio of about one to three, so that false positive rate and precision are estimated on a negative class larger than the positive one. The negatives comprise all distinct hard-ham records, chosen because they are the legitimate emails most likely to resemble phishing, together with a seeded sample of distinct Enron records to make up the total. The four variant conditions add 1,336 items, and the two controls add 668, for 3,340 items per detector per prompting strategy. The generalization set is a stratified, seeded sample of 2,000 English E-PhishLLM emails, 1,000 of each class, with the proportion of named to generic salutations preserved. Because the core set and the generalization set have different class prevalence, comparisons between them are made on measures that do not depend on prevalence, namely recall, false positive rate, and balanced accuracy; precision and accuracy are reported within each set with its prevalence stated.

A development set is held out for every design decision that involves looking at model behavior, so that no such decision is informed by test material. It consists of 400 items: 100 traditional phishing emails and 100 Enron emails from the training pool, and 100 personalized phishing emails and 100 legitimate emails from the English E-PhishLLM portion, all disjoint from the test set, the controls, and the generalization set. Prompt wording, output-format instructions, the paraphrase model's instructions, and the calibration of thresholds are developed on this set only and are frozen before any core or generalization item is processed. Few-shot examples, where a strategy uses them, are drawn from the traditional phishing and Enron collections and never from the spear-phishing originals or from E-PhishLLM. The generalization set is evaluated under the zero-shot condition and under one engineered strategy, chosen by a rule fixed in advance: the strategy with the highest macro-averaged F1 on the core test set. Because that choice is made on the core set and tested on a disjoint collection, it does not inflate the generalization result.

The conventional baselines follow the protocol of Nahmias et al. (2024): they are trained on the traditional phishing collection and on the Enron records not used in the test or development sets, with 10 percent held out for model selection, and tested on exactly the items the prompt-based detectors see, including the variants and controls. Training, development, and test material are disjoint by construction and verified by the near-duplicate test of Section 3.2.2. All sampling uses a single documented seed, and the identifiers of every selected record are released with the code, so that the exact composition can be reconstructed.
